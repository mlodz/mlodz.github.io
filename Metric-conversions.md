# Metric Conversions

## Temperature: Learning to Think in Celsius

### Everyday Benchmarks

| °C | Benchmark | °F |
|---:|-----------|---:|
| -18 | Bitter cold — dangerously cold | 0 |
| -10 | Brutal winter day | 14 |
| 0 | **Freezing point** | 32 |
| 4 | Fridge temp / beer serving temp | 39 |
| 10 | Cool fall day, light jacket | 50 |
| 12 | "Need a coat?" threshold | 54 |
| 15 | Mild — jeans and a sweater | 59 |
| 20 | **Comfortable room temperature** | 68 |
| 25 | Warm, pleasant day | 77 |
| 27 | Pool water | 80 |
| 30 | Hot summer day | 86 |
| 35 | Oppressively hot | 95 |
| 37 | **Body temperature** | 98.6 |
| 40 | Dangerously hot / high fever | 104 |

### Baking Temperatures

| °C | Benchmark | °F |
|---:|-----------|---:|
| 120 | Low & slow (dehydrating, meringues) | 250 |
| 150 | Slow roast / cheesecake | 300 |
| 160 | Slow-roast pulled pork | 325 |
| 180 | **Standard baking** (cakes, cookies, casseroles) | 350 |
| 190 | Banana bread, muffins | 375 |
| 200 | Roast chicken, vegetables | 400 |
| 220 | Pizza, high-heat roasting | 425 |
| 230 | Crusty bread | 450 |
| 260 | Broil / Neapolitan pizza | 500 |

### Mental Shortcuts

- **"Double and add 30"** — quick Celsius to Fahrenheit for everyday temps. 20°C → 40+30 = 70°F (actual 68). Good enough from 0–35°C.
- **Three anchors**: 0 = freezing, 20 = room temp, 37 = body temp.
- **Each 1°C ≈ 2°F** — so a 5° swing in Celsius feels like 10° in Fahrenheit.
- **Baking anchor**: 180°C = 350°F — the most common oven setting. Go up/down by 10°C for each ~25°F step.

## Distance: Learning to Think in Meters and Kilometers

### Everyday Distances

| Metric | Benchmark | Imperial |
|-------:|-----------|:---------|
| 1 cm | Width of a pencil | ~⅜ inch |
| 2.5 cm | **1 inch exactly** | 1 inch |
| 10 cm | Width of a fist | ~4 inches |
| 30 cm | Roughly a ruler / foot-long sub | ~1 foot |
| 1 m | One big step / kitchen counter height | 3 ft 3 in |

### Walking & Driving Distances

| Metric | Benchmark | Imperial |
|-------:|-----------|:---------|
| 400 m | Once around a track | ¼ mile |
| 800 m | **~10 minute walk** | ½ mile |
| 1 km | ~12 minute walk | 0.6 miles |
| 1.6 km | **1 mile exactly** | 1 mile |
| 5 km | Casual run / parkrun | 3.1 miles |
| 10 km | ~2 hour walk / quick bike ride | 6.2 miles |
| 42 km | Marathon | 26.2 miles |
| 100 km | ~1 hour highway drive | 62 miles |

### Body Height (Australians use cm)

| cm | Benchmark | ft/in |
|---:|-----------|:------|
| 152 | Short (women) | 5'0" |
| 160 | Average woman (Aus) | 5'3" |
| 165 | | 5'5" |
| 170 | Tall woman / short man | 5'7" |
| 175 | Average man (Aus) | 5'9" |
| 180 | Tall | 5'11" |
| 183 | **6 feet exactly** | 6'0" |
| 188 | Very tall | 6'2" |
| 193 | Basketball tall | 6'4" |

### Speed (road signs are in km/h)

| km/h | Benchmark | mph |
|-----:|-----------|----:|
| 40 | School zone | 25 |
| 50 | City streets | 31 |
| 60 | Suburban roads | 37 |
| 80 | Secondary highways | 50 |
| 100 | **Highway / freeway** | 62 |
| 110 | Fast freeway | 68 |

### Mental Shortcuts

- **Km to miles**: multiply by 0.6 (or divide by 1.6). 10 km ≈ 6 miles.
- **Cm to feet**: 30 cm ≈ 1 foot. For height, 152 cm = 5'0" then add ~2.5 cm per inch.
- **Km/h to mph**: drop 40%. 100 km/h → 60 mph.

## Weight: Learning to Think in Kilograms

### Everyday Weights

| Metric | Benchmark | Imperial |
|-------:|-----------|:---------|
| 5 g | A teaspoon of sugar | ~0.2 oz |
| 28 g | **1 ounce exactly** | 1 oz |
| 100 g | A deck of cards / small apple | 3.5 oz |
| 200 g | A steak at the butcher | 7 oz |
| 250 g | Half a pound (close enough) | 8.8 oz |
| 454 g | **1 pound exactly** | 1 lb |
| 500 g | Standard pack of mince / pasta | 1.1 lbs |
| 1 kg | **A bag of sugar / a litre of water** | 2.2 lbs |
| 2 kg | A rack of ribs | 4.4 lbs |
| 5 kg | A bag of potatoes | 11 lbs |
| 7 kg | Airline carry-on limit (some airlines) | 15 lbs |
| 23 kg | Standard checked bag limit | 50 lbs |

### Body Weight (scales, gyms, and doctors use kg)

| kg | Benchmark | lbs |
|---:|-----------|----:|
| 45 | Small/petite adult | 99 |
| 55 | | 121 |
| 60 | Average woman (Aus) | 132 |
| 68 | **150 lbs exactly** | 150 |
| 70 | | 154 |
| 75 | Average man (Aus) | 165 |
| 80 | | 176 |
| 82 | **180 lbs exactly** | 180 |
| 90 | Large build / athletic | 198 |
| 91 | **200 lbs exactly** | 200 |
| 100 | Heavy / tall & muscular | 220 |
| 110 | | 242 |

### Mental Shortcuts

- **Kg to lbs**: double it, then add 10%. 70 kg → 140 + 14 = 154 lbs (exact: 154.3).
- **Lbs to kg**: halve it, then subtract 10%. 180 lbs → 90 - 9 = 81 kg (exact: 81.6).
- **Quick anchor**: 1 kg ≈ 2.2 lbs. Just over double.
- **At the butcher/grocery**: 500 g ≈ 1 lb. If you'd buy "a pound of ground beef," ask for 500 grams.

## Volume: Learning to Think in Millilitres and Litres

### Drinks & Everyday Liquids

| Metric | Benchmark | Imperial |
|-------:|-----------|:---------|
| 5 mL | A teaspoon | 1 tsp |
| 15 mL | A tablespoon | 1 tbsp |
| 30 mL | A shot | 1 fl oz |
| 250 mL | **A glass of water / Aus measuring cup** | 8.5 fl oz |
| 330 mL | A can of soda | 11.2 fl oz (US can = 355 mL) |
| 375 mL | Aus stubby (beer bottle) | 12.7 fl oz |
| 500 mL | A small water bottle | ~1 pint |
| 750 mL | A wine bottle | 25.4 fl oz |
| 1 L | **A large water bottle / carton of milk** | 33.8 fl oz |
| 2 L | A big soda bottle | ~½ gallon |
| 3.8 L | **1 US gallon exactly** | 1 gallon |

### Cooking Volumes

| Metric | Benchmark | Imperial |
|-------:|-----------|:---------|
| 60 mL | ¼ Aus cup | ~¼ US cup |
| 80 mL | ⅓ Aus cup | ~⅓ US cup |
| 125 mL | ½ Aus cup | ~½ US cup |
| 250 mL | **1 Aus cup** (US cup = 237 mL) | ~1 US cup |
| 500 mL | 2 Aus cups | ~1 US pint |
| 1 L | 4 Aus cups | ~1 US quart |

### Australian Beer Sizes (varies by state!)

| Size | Where you'll hear it |
|-----:|----------------------|
| 200 mL | Butcher (SA) / Seven (TAS) |
| 285 mL | Pot (VIC/QLD) / Middy (NSW) / Half pint (SA) |
| 425 mL | **Schooner** (most states) — the standard beer |
| 570 mL | Pint (most states) / Imperial pint |

### Mental Shortcuts

- **Litres to gallons**: roughly 4 litres per gallon. A 60 L fuel tank ≈ 16 gallons.
- **mL to fl oz**: divide by 30. 250 mL ÷ 30 ≈ 8 oz.
- **Aus cup = 250 mL**, slightly bigger than a US cup (237 mL). Close enough for most recipes.
- **At the pub**: a schooner (425 mL) is a bit less than a US pint (473 mL). An Aus pint (570 mL) is bigger than a US pint.

## Formats & Conventions That'll Trip You Up

### Dates

| Context | Australia | United States |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| Written date | **DD/MM/YYYY** | MM/DD/YYYY |
| Example: June 3, 2026 | 03/06/2026 | 06/03/2026 |
| Ambiguous danger | 01/02/2026 = **1 Feb** | 01/02/2026 = **Jan 2** |
| ISO (tech/forms) | 2026-06-03 | 2026-06-03 |

Watch out: any date where both numbers are ≤ 12 will be silently misread. Double-check appointment confirmations, flight bookings, and form submissions.

### Time

| Context | Australia | United States |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| Casual speech | "Seven o'clock" / "seven PM" — same as US | "Seven o'clock" / "seven PM" |
| Saying 24h time aloud | "Nineteen hundred" or "nineteen oh oh" (never "nineteen o'clock") | Rarely used outside military |
| "Half seven" | Means **7:30** | Not commonly used |
| Written (schedules, doctors, events) | **19:00** (24h common) | 7:00 PM |
| Digital clocks/phones | Often default to 24h | Default to 12h |

24-hour time shows up on train timetables, restaurant bookings, event tickets, and official communications far more than in the US. But in everyday conversation people still say "seven" or "seven PM" — the 24h format is more written than spoken.

### Floor Numbering

| Level | Australia | United States |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| Street level | **Ground (G)** | 1st floor |
| One up | **Level 1** | 2nd floor |
| Two up | **Level 2** | 3rd floor |

Elevator buttons will say G, 1, 2, 3 — you're always "off by one" from US thinking.

### Phone Numbers

| Type | Australia | United States |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| Country code | **+61** | +1 |
| Mobile format | 04XX XXX XXX | (XXX) XXX-XXXX |
| Landline format | (0X) XXXX XXXX | (XXX) XXX-XXXX |

### Other Differences

| Thing | Australia | United States |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| Paper size | **A4** (210 × 297 mm) | Letter (8.5 × 11 in) |
| Mains electricity | **240V / 50Hz**, Type I plug | 120V / 60Hz, Type A/B plug |
| Number format | Same: 1,000.50 | Same: 1,000.50 |
| Spelling | British (**colour**, **organise**, **centre**) | American (color, organize, center) |

## How Australians Actually Say It

Units get shortened and dropped in everyday speech. If you say the full word you'll be understood, but you'll sound textbook-formal.

| What you'd say in the US | What Australians say | Example |
|--------------------------|----------------------|---------|
| "75 kilograms" | **"75 kilos"** | "I weigh about 80 kilos" |
| "10 kilometers" | **"10 kays"** (or "10 k's") | "It's about 10 kays from here" |
| "100 kilometers per hour" | **"100 kays an hour"** | "I was doing 110 kays an hour" |
| "35 degrees Celsius" | **"35 degrees"** | "It's gonna be 35 degrees tomorrow" |
| "180 centimeters" | **"180"** (no unit) | "Yeah, he's about 180" |
| "10 millimeters" | **"10 mil"** | "Hand me the 10 mil spanner" |
| "250 millilitres" | **"250 mils"** | "Add 250 mils of stock" |
| "litres" | **"litres"** (no shortening) | "Put 40 litres in" |

**The pattern**: Australians shorten everything they can (kilos, kays, mils) and drop units entirely when context makes them obvious — "I'm 180" for height, "it's 35 degrees" for weather, "doing 110" for driving speed. Celsius, centimeters, and km/h are all assumed.
